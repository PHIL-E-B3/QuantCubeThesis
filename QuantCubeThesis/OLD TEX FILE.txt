\textbf{Abstract}

This paper studies whether sentiment in Federal Open Market Committee communication helps forecast the future policy stance of the Federal Reserve. Using official statements, minutes, press conferences, and speeches, we compare transparent dictionary indicators with labels produced by a fine-tuned large language model. Combined with a macro-financial FAVAR benchmark, LLM-based sentiment substantially improves out-of-sample forecasts of the next-period shadow rate, while dictionary methods provide only limited gains. The strongest predictive content appears in interpretive, risk-oriented, and uncertainty-rich communication, suggesting that FOMC language matters because it reveals how policymakers assess conditions and the reaction function.



%We study whether linguistic signals in Federal Open Market Committee communication help forecast subsequent policy stance. We construct sentiment indicators from official statements and related texts using two approaches: domain dictionaries and large language models. These indicators are combined with standard macro-financial controls in forecasting regressions and classification models. We compare in-sample fit and out-of-sample predictive performance and assess economic interpretability. Results indicate that communication-based sentiment captures incremental information about policy direction and improves short-horizon forecasting under specific model designs.

\section{Introduction}
Does FOMC communication contain sentiment information that helps forecast the future policy stance of the Federal Reserve? FOMC communication is closely watched by investors because monetary policy works partly through expectations. The relevant question, however, is not simply whether language describes the decision made at a given meeting, but whether it contains information that is useful before the next decision is realised.

This paper studies whether sentiment extracted from public FOMC communication at meeting $t$ improves forecasts of the policy stance at $t+1$. Policy stance is measured using the shadow rate, which provides a continuous measure of monetary tightness, including during zero-lower-bound periods. The null hypothesis is that sentiment information cannot add predictability over policy stance once standard macro-financial controls are included. Rejecting this null would imply that FOMC language contains incremental information about the future direction or intensity of policy.

The timing of the forecast is central to the contribution. Much of the existing literature studies whether central bank language explains policy stance or market reactions at the same meeting date. This paper instead focuses on prediction. The analysis uses only information publicly available before the forecast target is realised and restricts the communication set to FOMC statements, press conferences, minutes, and speeches. This restriction is deliberate as the paper is inspired by applications to trading strategies. Hence, all text inputs must be observable to market participants at the time a forecast would be made.

The empirical strategy begins with dictionary-based sentiment indicators, which provide a transparent baseline and connect the analysis to earlier work that classifies or explains policy stance at time $t$. The paper then develops a large language model approach (LLM) by fine tuning Llama 3.1-8B to assign hawkish or dovish sentiment. The model was also trained to add labels for uncertainty, risk, tense, commitment, and topic. These additional labels are intended to capture features of communication that simple word counts miss, such as whether a sentence is descriptive or interpretive, whether it refers to tail risks, and whether it contains unconditional policy guidance. The minimum F1 score for any label was 0.778. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this richer annotation structure has not been used in this setting before.

Finally, the forecasting exercise combines the communication variables with a FAVAR-style macro-financial benchmark and evaluates performance in an expanding-window out-of-sample design. Variations of the sentiment indicators are tested for incremental predictive power over the next-period shadow rate by comparing their OOS forecast errors and RMSE against the macro-only baseline. The extra labels are also used as regime conditionals, allowing the analysis to ask when FOMC communication is most informative.

The main results show that LLM-based sentiment contains economically meaningful and statistically reliable information about future policy stance, and significantly outperforms existing dictionary approahces. The best LLM joint-PCA specification reduces out-of-sample RMSE by 14.1\% relative to the baseline model (significant at 1\%), while the best-performing dictionary approach (Sharpe) provides a reduction of 3.1\% only signficant at 10\%. By utilizing the additional labels as masks to investigate different regimes, the predictive content was found to be strongest in interpretive, conditional, and risk-oriented communication, suggesting that FOMC language is useful not because it mechanically reveals a private modal forecast, but because it encodes how policymakers assess uncertainty, risks, and the reaction function.

\section{Background Knowledge \& Literature Review}

% ============================================================

\subsection{History of FOMC Communication}

\subsubsection*{The Federal Reserve and its mandate}

The Federal Reserve was established in 1913 primarily as a lender of last
resort, with its early decades characterised by limited independence and a
mandate subordinated to government financing priorities, most acutely during
and after World War II, when the Fed was effectively directed to peg Treasury
yields. The Treasury-Fed Accord of 1951 restored a degree of operational
independence, but it was not until the Volcker disinflation of the early 1980s
that the modern conception of the Fed's dual mandate (maximum employment and
price stability) became the organising principle of monetary policy. The
\textit{Great Moderation} that followed, a period of declining macroeconomic
volatility running roughly from 1985 to 2007, was in large part attributed to
more credible and rule-consistent monetary policy \citep{bernanke2004great}. The
Global Financial Crisis of 2008 inaugurated a new regime: the federal funds rate
was cut to the zero lower bound (ZLB), and the Fed's balance sheet expanded
dramatically through successive rounds of large-scale asset purchases, requiring
the institution to develop new instruments of policy transmission beyond the
conventional interest rate channel.

\subsubsection*{From opacity to transparency}

For most of its history, the Federal Reserve communicated little and
deliberately so. The prevailing doctrine held that monetary policy was more
effective when markets could not anticipate it. This began to change in the
1990s, driven partly by the academic literature on central bank transparency
\citep{blinder2008central} and partly by practical necessity. In February 1994,
the FOMC issued its first-ever post-meeting statement, prompted by the decision
to raise interest rates for the first time in five years; Chairman Greenspan
judged that an unannounced hike would cause unnecessary market volatility. In
February 1995, the Committee formalised this practice by committing to announce
all changes in the stance of monetary policy immediately.

The content of communication deepened over subsequent years. In February 2000,
the FOMC began including in its statements an assessment of the balance of risks
facing the economy: an early form of forward guidance about the expected
direction of policy. In 2003, it added explicit guidance about the likely path of monetary policy. This evolution was directly influenced by
theoretical work on the role of expectations in monetary transmission. Eggertsson
and Woodford showed that when the policy rate is constrained at the zero lower
bound, a central bank can achieve additional accommodation by credibly committing
to keep rates low \textit{after} recovery has begun: if markets believe the
pledge, longer-term rates fall immediately in anticipation, stimulating demand
without any further rate cut \citep{eggertsson2003zero}. Communication, in this
framework, is not merely a description of policy; it is policy.

\subsubsection*{The forward guidance puzzle and its limits}

The theoretical power of forward guidance proved difficult to realise in
practice. \citet{delnegro2012forward} demonstrated in a standard New Keynesian
model that forward guidance grows more powerful the further into the future it
extends: a pledge to hold rates low for two or more years should, in the
model, produce near-explosive effects on inflation and output. This
\textit{forward guidance puzzle} arises because the model assumes that markets
perfectly internalise and believe the central bank's commitment, causing
long-term rates to adjust immediately and fully. Empirically, however, the Fed's ability to shift market expectations is found
to diminish the further into the future the guidance extends
\citep{campbelletal2012}, suggesting that
credibility is not unconditional and that markets apply a discount to distant
commitments.

\subsubsection*{Odyssean versus Delphic forward guidance}

This tension between the theoretical potency and empirical limitations of
forward guidance gives rise to a fundamental distinction in its practice.
\textit{Odyssean} forward guidance refers to explicit, unconditional commitments
about the future path of policy; the central bank ties itself to the mast,
deliberately removing its own discretion in order to manage expectations
\citep{campbelletal2012}. \textit{Delphic} forward guidance, by contrast, is
probabilistic and conditional: the central bank communicates its assessment of
the economic outlook and the policy response it \textit{expects} to follow,
without binding itself to a specific path.

The post-GFC period illustrates both the appeal and the dangers of the Odyssean
approach. Faced with a ZLB constraint and insufficient accommodation, the FOMC
adopted a \textit{lower for longer} posture, making explicit calendar-based and
threshold-based commitments to hold rates near zero. This succeeded in
compressing the yield curve and providing additional stimulus. Yet the same
credibility that made the commitments effective became an impediment when
conditions changed. In 2021--22, policymakers who had committed to a prolonged
accommodative stance delayed the necessary tightening cycle even as inflation
accelerated, unwilling to abandon commitments whose credibility underpinned the
broader policy framework \citep{clarida2022fomc}. The episode made clear that
Odyssean guidance, precisely because it works by constraining future discretion,
becomes a liability when the economic environment shifts faster than the
commitment horizon.

The practical implication is that the FOMC has converged on Delphic guidance as
its default mode of communication. Explicit unconditional commitments are
reserved for exceptional circumstances; the overwhelming majority of FOMC
communication consists of conditional assessments of the economic outlook,
qualitative characterisations of risk, and data-dependent descriptions of the
Committee's reaction function, none of which constitute binding commitments
and all of which require interpretation rather than simple reading.

\subsubsection*{Implications for information extraction}

Two conclusions follow from this history that motivate the present paper.
First, FOMC communication is substantively informative: it encodes the
Committee's assessment of the economic outlook, its characterisation of risks,
and its intended policy reaction function. Second, the dominant mode of this
communication is Delphic: implicit, conditional, and deliberately ambiguous
in a way that resists mechanical extraction. Explicit Odyssean signals, when
they occur, are immediately incorporated into asset prices and yield curves by
financial markets and offer limited incremental forecasting value. The
information that remains to be extracted is the subtler signal embedded in the
texture of Delphic communication: which risks the Committee emphasises, how its
characterisation of the outlook shifts across meetings, and where its attention
is concentrated. Accessing this signal requires methods capable of reading
beyond the surface of the text; this is something the NLP literature has only recently
developed the tools to attempt.

\subsection{NLP Sentiment Indicators}

The extraction of sentiment and information from central bank communication has
attracted a substantial body of research, with methods ranging from hand-crafted
dictionaries to large generative language models. This section surveys the main
approaches in roughly chronological order, motivating the choice of method
adopted in this paper.

\subsubsection*{Dictionary methods}

The earliest and most widely adopted approach constructs a domain-specific
lexicon of words associated with hawkish or dovish monetary policy stances.
Each document is scored by counting occurrences of positive and negative
keywords, typically normalised by document length. The method is transparent,
computationally inexpensive, and fully replicable, which explains its persistence
in the literature despite significant conceptual limitations.

The most influential general-purpose financial dictionary is that of
\citet{loughranmcdonald2011}, which demonstrated that standard general-language
sentiment dictionaries perform poorly on financial text because words like
\textit{liability} or \textit{risk} carry negative connotations in everyday
language but are neutral or positive in financial context. Domain-specific
dictionaries built directly on FOMC language address this further.
\citet{sharpe2023} construct a statement-level hawkishness index from
FOMC statements that tracks policy expectations closely over several decades.
\citet{gardner2022} and \citet{aruoba2022} develop related lexica applied to
broader corpora of FOMC communication, with \citeauthor{aruoba2022} showing
that their natural-language measure of monetary policy shocks recovers
well-identified surprises from high-frequency financial data.

The fundamental limitation of dictionary methods is that they are context-blind:
the same word receives the same score regardless of the sentence structure
surrounding it. A phrase like \textit{inflation expectations remain well
anchored} and \textit{inflation expectations are no longer anchored} would score
identically on a keyword count of \textit{inflation} and \textit{expectations},
yet convey diametrically opposite signals. This insensitivity to negation,
qualification, and syntactic structure motivates the move to more expressive
methods.

\subsubsection*{Classical machine learning methods}

A second generation of methods improves on dictionaries by learning statistical
patterns from text rather than hard-coding word lists. The standard pipeline
first converts documents into numerical vectors using bag-of-words (where
each dimension of the vector corresponds to a word in the vocabulary and records
how many times that word appears in the document) or term frequency-inverse
document frequency (TF-IDF) representations, which modify this count by
down-weighting terms that appear across most documents.

These vectors can then be used in unsupervised or supervised models. Latent
Dirichlet Allocation \citep[LDA;][]{blei2003lda}, a generative probabilistic
topic model, decomposes a corpus into latent topics represented as distributions
over words. \citet{hansenmcmahon2018} apply LDA to FOMC transcripts and show
that communication about the \textit{economic outlook} moves markets more than
communication about policy decisions directly. Supervised models, such as random
forests, have similarly been used to recover policy signals from central bank
texts \citep{deroux2023}.

Although these methods are more flexible than dictionaries, they still largely
rely on bag-of-words representations. As a result, word order, syntax, negation,
and long-range sentence dependencies are only weakly captured, motivating the
shift toward transformer-based approaches.

\subsubsection*{Transformer-based methods}

The introduction of transformer architectures \citep{vaswani2017attention}
fundamentally changed the possibilities for NLP. Rather than treating documents
as unordered bags of words, transformers process text sequentially, learning
context-sensitive representations of each token by attending to all other tokens
in the input. A word's representation therefore depends on its syntactic and
semantic context, enabling the model to distinguish negation, qualification, and
conditional structure that is invisible to dictionary or bag-of-words approaches.

Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers
\citep[BERT;][]{devlin2019bert} pre-trains a deep transformer on large text
corpora and produces contextualised token embeddings that can be fine-tuned for
downstream classification tasks. \citet{filippou2023fomc} apply BERT to FOMC
transcripts and Tealbook documents and find that text-based measures of
\textit{risk attentiveness} and \textit{sentiment} extracted with BERT add
value in the tails of the distribution of GDP and unemployment outcomes (evidence that the model captures signals that point forecasts and dictionary
methods do not).

Autoregressive large language models (LLMs) (most prominently the GPT family) extend the transformer paradigm to generation, producing coherent multi-token
outputs conditioned on a prompt. Applied to financial NLP, LLMs can be instructed
to classify or annotate text in a single inference call, producing structured
outputs that would require multiple independent classifiers under an encoder-only
architecture. Their chief practical limitation is cost: proprietary frontier
models such as GPT-4 charge per-token API fees that make sentence-level
annotation of a multi-decade corpus of FOMC documents economically prohibitive.

\subsubsection*{Efficient fine-tuning: LoRA}

Open-weight models such as Meta's Llama family offer an alternative: they can be
run locally and adapted to a specific task through fine-tuning. Full fine-tuning
of an 8-billion-parameter model, however, requires updating all parameters
simultaneously, demanding upwards of 60~GB of GPU memory, making it
inaccessible on standard research hardware.

Low-Rank Adaptation \citep[LoRA;][]{hu2021lora} resolves this by freezing the
pre-trained model and adding a small number of trainable adapter weights inside
selected transformer layers. Instead of rewriting the whole model, fine-tuning
only teaches these adapters how to adjust the model's behaviour for the target
task. The original Llama parameters remain fixed, while the adapters learn the
task-specific correction needed for FOMC sentence annotation. This reduces the
number of trainable parameters to under one per cent of the full model while
achieving performance comparable to full fine-tuning on classification tasks
\citep{hu2021lora}.

This makes it feasible to fine-tune a competitive open-weight LLM with
substantially lower hardware requirements than full fine-tuning, enabling
domain-specific adaptation at a fraction of the cost of API-based annotation.
This is the approach adopted in the present paper.

\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:forecasting_framework}

The dependent variable is the one-meeting-ahead change in the shadow federal funds rate,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shadow_change}
  \Delta r_{t+1} = r_{t+1} - r_t,
\end{equation}
where $r_t$ denotes the Wu-Xia shadow federal funds rate at meeting $t$ \citep{wuxia2016}. The shadow rate is used because the objective is to forecast the stance of monetary policy, not only the observed target rate. Outside zero-lower-bound periods, the shadow rate closely tracks the effective federal funds rate. During zero-lower-bound episodes, however, the observed rate is mechanically constrained near zero even when policy becomes more or less accommodative through forward guidance, asset purchases, or other balance-sheet tools. The shadow rate provides a single short-rate-equivalent proxy for this broader stance. The dependent variable is specified as the change rather than the level because interest rates are highly persistent: a level forecast can appear accurate simply by reproducing the current rate, whereas the change focuses the test on whether communication helps predict the next policy adjustment.

The sample begins in 2007 to focus on the modern transparency regime of Federal Reserve communication. Ben Bernanke replaced Alan Greenspan as Chair in early 2006, marking a shift toward more systematic and explicit communication. Starting the forecasting sample in 2007 allows the analysis to concentrate on this post-Greenspan communication environment, rather than mixing it with the earlier, more opaque style of FOMC communication.

The benchmark model is a Factor-Augmented Vector Autoregression (FAVAR) in the spirit of \citet{bernanke2005favar}. Standard macro-financial controls are first transformed into principal components and then used in a linear forecasting regression. The baseline specification is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:favar}
  \Delta r_{t+1} = \alpha + \boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_t + \varepsilon_{t+1},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{F}_t$ is the vector of PCA factors at meeting $t$. The PCA input set includes the deviation of CPI inflation from the 2 per cent target, the unemployment gap, GDP growth, CFNAI (Chicago Fed National Activity Index), the VIX, the shadow rate at time t, and the market-implied federal funds rate (following the approach of \citet{kuttner2001}). All principal components are retained so that the transformation removes multicollinearity without discarding macro-financial variation that may be relevant for assessing the incremental contribution of sentiment.

Two simpler benchmarks were also considered. The first is a naive model that always predicts no change in the policy stance. The second is a market-based model: the gap between the market-implied federal funds rate and the current shadow rate is used as the expected change in policy stance.

Since the motivation of the exercise is partly a trading application, the relevant criterion is not only in-sample fit but real-time forecasting performance. Because FOMC decisions are infrequent, a lower total RMSE over many meetings is only marginally informative on its own: it could be driven by a small number of unusually large errors. The evaluation therefore prioritises the sequence of out-of-sample forecast errors produced meeting by meeting under an expanding-window design.

In this context, a baseline is said to underperform when it has a larger out-of-sample RMSE than the FAVAR and does not deliver a statistically significant improvement in meeting-level prediction loss. Diebold--Mariano tests \citep{dieboldmariano1995} compare the squared forecast error of two models at each out-of-sample meeting and test whether the average loss differential is distinguishable from zero. This directly asks whether one model is systematically more accurate than another on a meeting-to-meeting basis, rather than merely whether its aggregate RMSE is lower.

Both simpler benchmarks underperform the FAVAR baseline under the preferred shadow-rate target, so the FAVAR is retained for the main comparisons. When the dependent variable is instead defined as the change in the effective federal funds rate, the market baseline produces a lower RMSE than the naive and FAVAR baselines, although the improvement is not statistically significant (Appendix~\ref{app:baseline-comparison}). For the main specification, however, the shadow-rate target is preferred because it better captures the stance of policy during unconventional-policy periods.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:favar_baseline} reports the baseline FAVAR performance for the main shadow-rate-change specification. The expanding-window evaluation uses 151 post-2007 FOMC meetings, with 76 out-of-sample predictions after the initial 50\% training window, achieving an RMSE of 0.2826. The adjusted in-sample $R^2$ is 0.301.

Against this benchmark, the forecasting tests add communication variables to evaluate whether sentiment contains incremental predictive information. The main null hypothesis is that sentiment-based variables do not improve forecasts of $\Delta r_{t+1}$ relative to the macro-financial FAVAR baseline. Two types of comparisons are used. When sentiment variables are added directly to the macro-only regression, the sentiment-augmented specification nests the baseline, so forecast improvements are evaluated using nested-model tests such as Clark--West. When sentiment variables are instead included in the PCA step before the forecasting regression, the resulting factor structure differs from the macro-only FAVAR and the models are non-nested; these comparisons are therefore evaluated using Diebold--Mariano tests.

\section{Constructing Sentiment Indicator}
\label{sec:sentiment}
\subsection{Dictionary Approach}
Existing dictionaries will be used; however, the work is not redundant as they will be applied on a different corpora of text and the forecasting excercise has a different dependent variable. Several dictionary-based approaches could be used to construct FOMC sentiment indicators, including the Loughran--McDonald dictionary \citep{loughranmcdonald2011} and the measure developed by Aruoba and Drechsel \citep{aruoba2022}. In this paper, however, the dictionary analysis focuses on two alternatives: the Sharpe dictionary \citep{sharpe2023} and the Gardner dictionary \citep{gardner2022}. These two dictionaries were retained because, in preliminary tests, they were the only dictionary specifications that improved in-sample fit relative to the macro-financial baseline.

  %─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  \subsubsection{Sharpe Dictionary}
  \label{sec:sharpe}
  %─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  \citet{sharpe2023} construct a net tone measure by matching words in a
  document against two fixed lists: one associated with economic strength and
  optimism, and one associated with weakness or stress. The core idea is simple:
  positive words increase the score, while negative words decrease it.

  The score is computed by counting how many words in the document appear in
  the positive list and how many appear in the negative list. The Sharpe
  dictionary score is then the positive count minus the negative count. Higher
  values therefore indicate more optimistic or hawkish language, while lower
  values indicate weaker or more dovish language.

  \paragraph{Worked example.}
  Consider the following FOMC statement excerpt:
  \vspace{-0.4\baselineskip}
  \begin{quote}
    ``Consumer spending grew at a \underline{solid} pace supported by
    \underline{stable} labour market conditions despite \underline{sluggish}
    export demand.''
  \end{quote}
  \vspace{-0.5\baselineskip}

  Token matching against the Sharpe lexicon identifies \textit{solid} and
  \textit{stable} as positive hits ($P = 2$) and \textit{sluggish} as a
  negative hit ($N = 1$). The net score is $(2 - 1) = 1$.

  The Sharpe method is fast and fully reproducible but treats every token
  independently of syntax, topic, and context: the word \textit{low}
  receives no score even if the phrase is ``low unemployment''
  (unambiguously a positive signal).

  %─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  \subsubsection{Gardner Dictionary}
  \label{sec:gardner}
  %─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  \citet{gardner2022} construct sentiment scores at the sentence level rather
  than by counting isolated words. The core idea is to identify language related
  to five policy-relevant topics: inflation, the labour market, real output,
  financial conditions, and explicit monetary policy. Within each topic, the
  sentiment of a sentence depends on the relevant economic keyword and its nearby
  modifier. Gardner et al. use curated dictionaries of topic keywords and
  modifiers, where some topic words also carry an inherent sign: for example,
  unemployment is negative for the labour-market topic.

  The advantage of the Gardner dictionary is that it preserves topic-level
  information. Rather than producing a single document-level word-count measure,
  it separates inflation, labour, output, financial, and monetary-policy signals
  before aggregating them. This is useful for the forecasting exercise because
  different types of hawkish or dovish language may have different implications
  for the future policy stance.

  \paragraph{Worked example.}
  Consider the following two-sentence excerpt:
  \vspace{-0.4\baselineskip}
  \begin{quote}
    \newcommand{\underlabel}[2]{\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}c@{}}\underline{#1}\\[-1.5ex]{\scriptsize #2}\end{tabular}}
    ``The \underlabel{labour market}{topic} showed \underlabel{improvement}{modifier}, with
    \underlabel{declining}{modifier} \underlabel{unemployment}{topic}.
  \end{quote}
  \vspace{-0.5\baselineskip}

  The first topic term, \textit{labour market}, is paired with the nearby
  positive modifier \textit{improvement}, giving a positive labour-market
  signal. The second topic term, \textit{unemployment}, has a negative inherent
  sign, but it is paired with \textit{declining}; the two negatives imply an
  improving labour-market signal. The aggregate Gardner score combines these
  topic-level signals, while the separate sub-indexes can also be used
  individually in the forecasting regressions.

\subsection{LLM Approach}
This subsection describes the paper's main methodological contribution to NLP: an LLM-based annotation pipeline that moves beyond dictionary word counts by assigning sentence-level labels for sentiment, uncertainty, risk, tense, commitment, and topic.

\subsubsection{Corpus Construction}

\paragraph{Data collection and pre-processing.}
Five document types are collected from the Federal Reserve's public website:
FOMC \textit{statements}, \textit{minutes}, \textit{speeches},
\textit{press conference prepared remarks}, and \textit{press conference Q\&A
transcripts}. After standard pre-processing (Unicode normalisation, encoding
artefact removal, truncation of procedural appendices), documents are tokenised
into candidate sentences using NLTK's Punkt algorithm
\citep{kiss2006unsupervised}, which learns to distinguish sentence-ending
periods from abbreviations (\textit{U.S.}, \textit{p.m.}, \textit{Fed.})
without custom training. A regex-based boilerplate filter (essentially a dictionary approach) then removes sentences
with no policy-relevant content, including voting records, administrative
directives, and generic data-dependence formulations such as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{``Voting for this action: Jerome H.\ Powell, Chair; John C.\
        Williams, Vice Chair\ldots''}
\end{itemize}
\noindent The filter was devised iteratively after analyzing many FOMC documents and is intentionally conservative: an estimated 15--20\% of
surviving sentences still carry no policy signal and are subsequently labelled
\texttt{no\_topic} by the annotator. This is needed to avoid losing any relevant sentences to the hard-coded rules of the regex-based filter.

\paragraph{Unit of analysis.}
The fundamental design choice is the unit at which sentiment is extracted. The
ideal unit is a \textit{thought}: the minimum self-contained idea that a
speaker is communicating about the economy or policy. Existing dictionary-based
studies show that FOMC language contains information relevant for policy and
financial-market outcomes, but they usually recover this information from word
counts aggregated over larger blocks of text. The LLM approach adopted here is
intended to preserve more of the structure inside those blocks. It matters not
only whether a document sounds hawkish or dovish overall, but also which topics
receive attention, how strong individual assessments are, and whether risks or
uncertainty are being emphasised.

Extracting sentiment only at the document level would hide this internal
variation. A full statement or set of minutes can contain several distinct
messages, and asking the model to assign one label to a more-than-1000-word
document would force it to compress conflicting signals into a single output.
Paragraph-level extraction is also too coarse, since a single FOMC paragraph can
contain several analytically separable assessments.

The sentence is therefore adopted as the operational approximation of the
thought-level ideal, with document-level indicators constructed ex post as aggregates over sentence-level labels (explored further in section~\ref{sec:results}). This is an
acknowledged imperfect operationalisation: some sentences contain more than one
distinct idea, and some are grammatically incomplete without their predecessor.
The former case is accepted as annotation noise; the latter is addressed by a
targeted merging step.

Sentences that open with a formal connective (\textit{consequently},
\textit{therefore}) or a demonstrative noun phrase (\textit{this assessment},
\textit{this policy}) have no recoverable referent in isolation and would yield
unreliable labels if submitted independently. Such sentences are merged with
their predecessor into a single annotation unit. The following example
illustrates:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``"We all monitor the labor market very, very carefully. And I don't see \textbf{those cracks} today.''}
\end{quote}

\noindent Submitted in isolation, the second sentence has no recoverable
subject; the two are merged into a single annotation unit, enriching both sentences. Merging is capped at
three sentences and 1,000 characters to prevent runaway chains, and is
conservative by design: the goal is to recover the minimum context needed for a
well-defined label, not to reconstruct full paragraphs.

\subsubsection{Architecture of FOMC Communication \& Labelling Scheme}
\label{sec:Architecture}
The labelling scheme is designed around the central question of this thesis:
what information does the FOMC communicate, and how is that information encoded
in language? The literature review showed that the FOMC uses forward guidance
and that speakers attempt to convey information about the expected economic
outlook. This implies the obvious starting point that FOMC communication
contains policymakers' views about future economic outcomes.

The first instinct
is therefore to think of central bank communication as a forecast-revision
channel: the FOMC may have better information than private institutions because
it observes more data, and by communicating its stance through forward guidance,
it leads private agents to update their own projections.

We tested this interpretation directly by using the wedge between Tealbook and
Survey of Professional Forecasters projections (public minus private
expectations) as a regressor in the baseline FAVAR. The results were statistically insignificant for all OOS metrics (see Appendix~\ref{sec:Wedge}). This suggests that the predictive content of communication is not explained by a simple correction of private-sector modal forecasts.

The result is
surprising because the dictionary literature finds that hawkish--dovish language
can have predictive power, and dictionary methods are, at a high level, little
more than counts of hawkish and dovish sentences. Those counts should mostly reflect the
same modal-outlook information captured by the Tealbook--SPF wedge, which we do
not find to be significant.

The key observation is that dictionary labels are assigned at the sentence level,
whereas the forecasting regressor is constructed at the document level. Moving
from sentences to documents is not a neutral operation. If the document-level
measure is an additive count or share of labelled sentences, then it is not only
measuring the direction of individual statements; it is also measuring
\textit{attention}. The amount of text devoted to a topic reveals how worried
the Fed is about that topic, or how important the Committee believes it is. This
interpretation is new in our setting but is consistent with the view that
text-based monetary-policy measures capture risk attentiveness rather than only
the central forecast \citep{aruoba2022}. The implication for the LLM design is
that the schema should preserve not only hawkish or dovish tone, but also the
distributional structure of the information being communicated: modal outcomes,
tail risks, uncertainty, interpretation, and policy commitments.

\paragraph{Policy sentiment.}
In the spirit of existing dictionary approaches, each sentence
is assigned a hawkish, neutral, or dovish policy orientation. This label remains
useful because it captures the direction of the policy implication and, as discussed, can provide a measure of attention.

The motivation for using an
LLM, however, is that sentence-level sentiment is often not recoverable from
keywords alone. For example, the sentence ``One participant, however, expressed
concern that considerable uncertainty surrounded the relationship between
unemployment and inflation, raising questions about the extent to which resource
slack would keep inflation restrained over the medium term'' contains words such
as \textit{concern}, \textit{uncertainty}, \textit{resource slack}, and
\textit{restrained}, which a dictionary may treat as dovish. The
sentence is hawkish because it questions whether the usual Phillips-curve
mechanism will operate: the participant is worried that slack may not keep
inflation down. The policy implication depends on the argument made by the
sentence, not on the polarity of isolated words.

This example also clarifies the limits of the dictionary benchmark. In theory, an
extremely curated dictionary could classify many of these constructions
correctly. The claim is not that a dictionary cannot do the task in principle.
Rather, the relevant grammar is too varied for a finite manually curated rule set
to be practical. Capturing mechanism inversions, implicit hypotheticals, scope
limitations, and conditional commitments would require a large number of
hand-coded rules, creating a high risk of overfitting to observed phrasing. The
work required would be disproportionate to the gain, whereas an LLM can apply the
same underlying semantic judgement across many forms.

\paragraph{Risk balance.}
The preceding logic motivates adding a sentence-level risk-balance label. Instead of relying purely on aggregation to extract tail-risk, each sentence can be evaluated for tail risk independently. The risk-balance
label identifies whether a sentence describes risks as \textit{skewed upside},
\textit{skewed downside}, \textit{symmetric}, or \textit{na}. This flag is not
intended to capture only explicit mentions of the word ``risk''. It is designed
to identify concerns about specific adverse outcomes, including risks expressed
through hypothetical or conditional grammar. For example, the sentence ``the
indirect effects of the commodity price surge could be amplified substantially
if longer-run inflation expectations started drifting upward'' describes upside
inflation risk through a conditional chain of events. The value of the LLM is
precisely that it can recognise the risk structure even when no simple risk
keyword is present.

\paragraph{Width and uncertainty.}
Once FOMC information is interpreted as a distribution of possible outcomes, the
next question is whether the width of that distribution matters. At first, this
seems ambiguous: if the FOMC is equally uncertain in both directions, then the
upside and downside forces should cancel out. That conclusion depends, however,
on a symmetric policy reaction function. If policymakers are more concerned
about upside inflation than downside inflation, then uncertainty itself can move
the expected policy response because the possibility of high inflation receives
greater weight. This is consistent with a risk-management view of monetary
policy, in which policymakers care not only about expected outcomes but also
about the risks surrounding those projections \citep{brainard1967uncertainty,bernanke2008inflationissues}.

The schema therefore includes a width label with two values: \textit{elevated}
and \textit{none}. The label marks sentences that indicate a wider or less
precisely known distribution of possible outcomes. As with risk balance, it is
not limited to explicit uncertainty words. A sentence can signal elevated width
by acknowledging that information is incomplete, measurement is imperfect, or a
relationship is not fully understood. For example, the sentence ``While these
studies allow for differences in the weighting of price indexes across different
income groups, they rely on the same elementary price indexes for subcategories
of goods and services. As a result, they may miss additional sources of
variation in the inflation rates experienced by households at different income
levels'' contains no obvious uncertainty keyword. The width label is elevated
because the sentence recognises a blind spot in the measurement tool. This is the
kind of implicit acknowledgement of unknown information that is difficult for a
dictionary but natural for an LLM.

\paragraph{Tense.}
The schema also distinguishes descriptive language from interpretive or
forward-looking language. Descriptive communication refers to past events or the
current state of the economy; interpretive communication refers to judgements
about future developments or the meaning of observed data. In principle,
descriptive information should already be priced by markets and should therefore
have limited predictive value. Interpretive language may be more informative
because it reveals how policymakers map observed data into future outcomes and
policy decisions. This distinction is not reducible to grammatical tense. For
example, ``Participants discussed financial market developments, including their
views on the extent to which the rise in longer-term interest rates since May
reflected growing confidence'' contains forward-sounding words such as
\textit{growing}, but it is descriptive because it discusses how participants
interpreted an already observed market move. The LLM is useful because it can
classify the economic role of the sentence rather than only its verb tense.

\paragraph{Commitment.}
The commitment label follows directly from the literature review's distinction
between Odyssean and Delphic forward guidance \citep{campbelletal2012}. We
expect most FOMC communication to be Delphic because the Committee generally
avoids risking credibility by making unconditional promises. At the same time,
the regression sample includes the zero-lower-bound period of the 2010s, when
the FOMC explicitly committed to keeping policy lower for longer. It is
therefore useful to separate unconditional commitments from conditional or
outlook-based guidance. This also requires contextual judgement. For example,
``We remain committed to bringing inflation back down to our 2 percent goal and
to keep longer-term inflation expectations well anchored'' contains
commitment-like words, but the correct label is \textit{none} rather than
\textit{unconditional}: the Committee is expressing an objective, not committing
to a specific future policy action. This distinction between goals,
data-dependent guidance, and true commitments is exactly the type of
context-dependent classification that motivates using an LLM.

\paragraph{Topic.}
Finally, each sentence is assigned a topic label: \textit{inflation, labor-market, financial conditions, economic activity, macro and no topic}. These
topics are commonly used in the central-bank communication literature, except for \textit{macro} made for sentences that refer to the overall economic
outlook rather than to a single component such as inflation or economic
activity. Splitting communication by
topic serves two purposes. Economically, it allows the forecasting exercise to
ask which parts of FOMC communication are informative. Methodologically, the topic label helps the LLM
classify sentiment more consistently, because the same word can have different
policy implications depending on whether the sentence is about inflation,
employment, financial conditions, or the policy reaction function.

Taken together, these labels operationalise the thesis's core claim. FOMC
communication should not be treated only as a direct statement of the modal
forecast. The more informative object is the distribution of attention across
topics, risks, uncertainty, interpretive statements, and commitments. The LLM
schema is designed to preserve this structure at the sentence level before
constructing document-level regressors for the forecasting exercise. The full
annotation schema is reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:llm-label-schema}.

\vspace{-2mm}
\subsubsection{LLM Selection}
The annotation schema requires a structured 6-field JSON object for each
sentence, with several fields populated conditionally on the values of other
fields. This makes the task different from a standard single-label sentiment
classification problem. Encoder-only models such as RoBERTa Large are strong
classifiers, but they do not naturally emit a complete structured object in one
pass. Using RoBERTa would require separate classification heads for each field
or a sequence of field-specific models. That design would make the conditional
logic external to the model and would treat topic, sentiment, risk, uncertainty,
commitment, and horizon as largely independent outputs. A decoder-only
instruction-tuned model is better matched to the annotation problem because it
can generate the full JSON object directly and can condition later fields on the
fields already generated.

Among decoder-only models of comparable size, Llama~3.1 8B was selected over
alternatives such as Qwen~2.5 7B and Mistral~7B. Mistral~7B was not pursued
because more recent small open models offer stronger instruction following and
fine-tuning support. The relevant choice was therefore between Llama and Qwen. Qwen~2.5 has strong general structured-output capabilities, but the available
financial NLP evidence favours the Llama family for domain-specific sentiment
fine-tuning: a direct comparison of lightweight LLMs on heterogeneous financial
sentiment datasets finds that Llama~3 8B slightly but consistently outperforms
Qwen~3 8B in the fully fine-tuned setting \citep{chen2025finetuning}. Llama~3.1
8B also has a mature LoRA fine-tuning ecosystem and broad tooling support, which
reduced implementation risk for repeated active-learning and fine-tuning rounds.

 \subsubsection{LLM Training}
While Llama~3.1 8B comes pretrained on a large text corpus, it is not specialised in FOMC terminology or the annotation scheme used in this paper. Fine-tuning is therefore necessary, and the training data had to be created by manually labelling sentences before they could be fed into the model.

The training sample combines breadth with targeted coverage. To ensure that the model sees the full range of communication styles, random samples of 150 sentences were first drawn from each document type. However, several substantively important labels are rare in a purely random sample, including \textit{skewed\_upside}, \textit{elevated}, and \textit{unconditional}. These categories are precisely the ones that are hardest for the model to learn and most important for the later regime analysis. Additional examples were therefore manually appended until the rare labels had enough observations to be useful for fine-tuning.

This targeted sampling was done manually rather than by relying primarily on dictionary hits. A dictionary-based search would have overrepresented easy cases whose wording is already recognisable by fixed word lists. Manual search made it possible to include more varied and context-dependent examples, which is essential for training a model whose intended contribution is to move beyond dictionary rules. The final training set had 912 sentences.

  \paragraph{LoRA.}

  Llama~3.1 8B is fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation
  \citep[LoRA;][]{hu2021lora}, which freezes the base-model weights and
  injects trainable low-rank adapter matrices $\Delta W = BA$ into the
  transformer layers, with only $A$ and $B$ updated during training.
  The adapted forward pass is
  $h = W_0 x + \tfrac{\alpha}{r} BAx$,
  where $\alpha$ is a scaling hyperparameter.
  This reduces the trainable parameter count to under one per cent of the
  full model while keeping memory requirements within the 16~GB VRAM budget
  of a single consumer GPU.
  Adapters are injected across \emph{all} linear layers rather than
  attention matrices alone; this has the practical consequence that final
  performance becomes largely insensitive to the choice of rank $r$,
  shifting the primary regularisation burden to the remaining
  hyperparameters discussed below.

  \paragraph{Hyperparameter Optimisation.}

  Five hyperparameters govern the expressivity and regularisation of LoRA
  fine-tuning: rank $r$, scaling multiplier $m$ (where $\alpha = m \cdot r$),
  LoRA dropout, learning rate, and AdamW weight decay.
  Because the training corpus comprises approximately 900 annotated sentences,
  overfitting is the dominant risk; optimal values are not directly
  transferable from the large-dataset regimes studied in the bulk of the
  fine-tuning literature and must be determined empirically.
  Preliminary review of comparable financial NLP fine-tuning experiments on
  Llama~3.1 8B was used to establish principled bounds for each parameter,
  and systematic search was then performed using Optuna
  \citep{akiba2019optuna} with Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)
  sampling over 50 trials.

  \textit{Rank $r$.}
  The rank controls the dimensionality of the LoRA update matrices and, by
  extension, the number of trainable parameters introduced per layer.
  In a low-data regime the primary concern is that higher rank increases
  overfitting risk.
  The closest analogue to this study in the literature is FinLoRA
  \citep{finlora2024}, which applies LoRA fine-tuning of Llama~3.1 8B to
  financial classification tasks including sentiment analysis, and reports
  that rank 4 achieves performance comparable to rank 8 while requiring less
  memory.
  The search space $r \in \{2, 4, 8\}$ therefore spans from a minimally
  expressive adapter to the FinLoRA upper bound, with $r = 4$ as the
  informed prior.

  \textit{Alpha multiplier $m$.}
  The scaling parameter $\alpha$ is parameterised as $m \cdot r$ with
  $m \in \{1, 2\}$. A multiplier of 2 is empirically optimal across most
  tasks \citep{raschka2023lora}, and tuning $m$ jointly with the learning
  rate avoids confounding the two, since \citet{hu2021lora} note that
  adjusting $\alpha$ is broadly analogous to rescaling the learning rate.

  \textit{LoRA dropout.}
  Dropout applied to the adapter layers provides the primary regularisation
  channel.
  \citet{hu2021lora} recommend a rate of 0.05 for 7--8B models, but this
  baseline was established on large instruction datasets.
  In the low-data regime of this study, stronger regularisation may be
  warranted, and the search space $p \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$ extends
  upward from that baseline accordingly.

  \textit{Learning rate.}
  The learning rate is the most sensitive LoRA hyperparameter, with small
  differences producing large swings in task performance
  \citep{biderman2024lora}.
  It is searched on a log scale over $[10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-4}]$, with
  $2 \times 10^{-4}$ as the standard starting point in the literature
  \citep{hu2021lora, finlora2024}.

  \textit{Weight decay.}
  \citet{hu2021lora} note that applying weight decay to the LoRA matrices
  $A$ and $B$ implicitly decays updates back toward the pre-trained weights,
  providing a natural guard against catastrophic forgetting distinct from
  dropout.
  The search space $\lambda \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1\}$ treats weight decay
  as a second, independent regularisation axis alongside dropout.

  To avoid wasteful evaluation of poor configurations on a constrained GPU
  budget, the Optuna search employs a Median Pruner: the first 10 trials
  perform unconstrained random exploration to establish a performance
  baseline, after which any trial whose intermediate validation score falls
  below the median of completed trials at the same step is terminated early.
  The full search space is summarised in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:optuna_search}.


  \paragraph{Training Configuration.}

  Beyond the Optuna-optimised hyperparameters, a set of fixed training
  parameters governs the mechanics of the fine-tuning procedure, chosen
  to ensure stable convergence within the memory constraints of a single
  consumer GPU.

  Training proceeds for 2 epochs over the annotated corpus.
  The small training set places this study in a regime where the model
  risks memorising training examples rather than generalising; 2 epochs
  represents a conservative ceiling motivated by this constraint
  \citep{hu2021lora, finlora2024}.
  The effective batch size is set to 16, achieved via a per-device batch
  size of 8 with 2 gradient accumulation steps, within the range supported
  by 16~GB VRAM.
  A cosine learning rate schedule with a warmup ratio of 0.1 decays the
  learning rate smoothly from its peak to near zero, reducing the risk of
  large gradient updates disrupting pre-trained representations in the
  final steps \citep{raschka2023lora}.
  Training is performed in BFloat16 precision. The full configuration is reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:training_config}

\subsection{LLM Performance}
\paragraph{Prompt Engineering}

The prompt-engineering process iterated over three components: instruction
verbosity, the number and composition of few-shot examples, and whether to use
explicit chain-of-thought reasoning. These choices created a trade-off between
label quality and inference cost. Minimal prompts with few or no examples
performed poorly, with SumF1 close to 0.15, showing that the model could not
infer the annotation procedure from short definitions alone. Adding few-shot
examples produced the first meaningful improvement. The strongest early
candidates were P5 and P7: P5 used three carefully chosen examples and left
enough space to emphasise the most important labelling rules, while P7 used five
examples but necessarily compressed the instructions. Their performance was
close, and together they showed that verbosity and robust instructions were
central to performance, not merely auxiliary prompt details. P5 was slightly
more robust and became the baseline for subsequent iterations.

We then tested whether simply adding more information improved performance. It
did not. P8, which included twelve examples, and a variant based on the full
human annotation guide both performed worse than the shorter P5/P7-style
prompts. Chain-of-thought prompting produced the same conclusion: it helped some
individual labels, especially intensity and extreme dovish/hawkish cases, but
created offsetting errors in default categories such as \textit{neutral} and
increased inference time by roughly three to four times. These experiments made
clear that prompt length was itself a modelling constraint, not just an
implementation detail.

The final iteration therefore started from P5 rather than from the longer
variants. P5\_FINAL improved the balance between examples and instructions, but
still underperformed on rare and difficult labels. P5\_v10 addressed this by
adding examples for \textit{skewed\_downside} and unconditional commitment,
raising SumF1 to 0.626 and confirming the importance of few-shot prompting.
However, this version was too long for the production labelling pipeline: the
additional examples increased inference cost materially, while the marginal
validation gain was already beginning to plateau.

We therefore iterated once more by pruning rather than expanding the prompt.
This showed that width labels were robust to shorter instructions, whereas risk
labels required more detail because they combined two judgements: recognising a
phrase as tail risk and assigning its direction. The final P5-derived prompt kept
the stronger risk instructions, simplified the width instructions, and reduced
the number of examples relative to P5\_v10. It preserved most of the validation
gain while lowering inference cost enough for production labelling; the
remaining rare-label weaknesses were left for the fine-tuning stage. The final prompt can be seen in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}.

\paragraph{Evaluation}
The evaluation set follows the same logic as the training data. It starts from a random sample of 500 sentences and then manually appends additional rare-label examples until each relevant category contains at least 50 observations. This avoids evaluating the model mostly on common labels while still keeping the rare-label search independent of dictionary rules, so the benchmark methods are not mechanically favoured.

F1 scores are used as the primary evaluation metric because the label
distribution is uneven and the central evaluation problem is whether each method
can recover rare categories without over-firing them. For a given label,
precision is defined as
$TP/(TP+FP)$, where $TP$ denotes true positives and $FP$ denotes false positives;
it therefore measures how often predictions of that label are correct. Recall is
defined as $TP/(TP+FN)$, where $FN$ denotes false negatives; it measures how many
true instances of the label are recovered. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of
precision and recall,
\[
F1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}
{\text{precision} + \text{recall}},
\]
and is high only when the model performs well on both dimensions. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:active-learning-performance} summarises performance across the different models for all labels. Because the Sharpe and Gardner dictionaries produce only sentence-level sentiment scores, their evaluation is limited to the sentiment dimension. To make them comparable with the LLM labels, each dictionary score is converted into a three-class sentiment label using a simple thresholding rule: positive net scores are classified as hawkish, negative net scores as dovish, and zero net scores as neutral. Ultimately, the fine-tuned model is significantly better than the rest, having achieved a sentiment F1 score of 0.778 (0.118 higher than the non fine tuned model). Meanwhile the Gardner dictionary (best dictionary) only achieved 0.460.

\section{Forecasting Future Policy Stance}
\label{sec:results}
This section presents the out-of-sample (OOS) forecasting results for the three sentiment methods  (LLM, Gardner, and Sharpe) against a macro-only FAVAR baseline. The dependent variable throughout is $\Delta\text{EffRate}_{t+1} = \text{EffRate}_{t+1} - \text{EffRate}_t$, the one-meeting-ahead change in the shadow federal funds rate. All OOS estimates use an expanding window initialised at 50\% of the sample ($N=151$ FOMC meetings, 2007--2025), yielding 76 OOS predictions. The baseline is a macro-only FAVAR with principal components from seven macro-financial regressors (inflation deviation, unemployment gap, GDP growth, VIX, CFNAI, implied FFR, effective rate).

% ============================================================
%  4.1  Predictive correlations
% ============================================================

\subsection{Predictive Correlations}

In the next section, several sentiment indicators and models will be introduced. In this section, the typical definition is retained as a an aggregate sum of sentence scores. These scores are then normalized by dividing by the wordcount and applying an expanding z-score. This makes the scores comparable.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:correlations} reports correlations of the sentiment indicators with the dependent variable. The LLM aggregate sentiment score achieves a Pearson correlation of $r = 0.297$ ($p < 0.001$), just below inflation sentiment ($r = 0.303$), and surpasses it on Spearman rank correlation ($\rho = 0.327$ vs $0.140$). Gardner's aggregate carries a modest but significant signal ($r = 0.141$, $\rho = 0.248$). Sharpe's net sentiment is indistinguishable from zero on both metrics. These raw correlations motivate the subsequent model comparison but do not control for the macro baseline.

Figures~\ref{fig:llm_ts}--\ref{fig:sharpe_ts} plot each sentiment indicator alongside $\Delta\text{EffRate}_{t+1}$ over the full sample. The LLM series tracks the sign and magnitude of rate changes closely, with sharp negative readings preceding the 2008--2009 easing cycle, and a pronounced hawkish spike in 2022. Gardner's signal is directionally consistent but flatter and misses several turning points. Sharpe's series shows a less correlated signal where, although major events like the 2022 hike is captured, smaller deviations are not. A defining feature of all series, but perhaps LLM most, is high volatility. This will be addressed later.

% ============================================================
%  4.2  Testing framework
% ============================================================

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Forecasting Performance}

Rather than testing a single model, we evaluate progressively richer integration strategies against the same expanding-window macro FAVAR baseline. For steps 4a--4g, the comparison is nested because the baseline is augmented with an additional candidate regressor block $Z_t$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\text{EffRate}_{t+1} = \alpha + \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{F}_t + \boldsymbol{\gamma}'Z_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}.
\end{equation}
The object being swapped across specifications 4a--4h is therefore $Z_t$: total sentiment, dispersion, consensus, topic scores, topic PCA factors, interaction terms, novelty, or smoothed sentiment. Step 4i is different because it recomputes PCA jointly on macro and sentiment inputs, changing the factor space itself and therefore producing a non-nested model.

\textbf{4a — Aggregate sentiment.} Calculates total meeting sentiment as the sum of sentence-level sentiment scores, $\displaystyle S_t=\sum_i s_{i,t}$, where hawkish sentences enter positively and dovish sentences enter negatively. This is the simplest test of whether overall FOMC tone adds information beyond the macro baseline.

\textbf{4b1 — Sentiment dispersion.} Calculates the within-meeting standard deviation of sentence sentiment, $\operatorname{sd}(s_{i,t})$. This tests whether disagreement, ambiguity, or mixed communication is informative even when the average tone is unchanged.

\textbf{4b2 — Consensus.} Calculates the absolute value of mean meeting sentiment, $|\bar{s}_t|$. This measures how strongly communication leans in one direction, regardless of whether the direction is hawkish or dovish.

\textbf{4c — Topic-specific sentiment.} Calculates separate sentiment sums by topic, $\displaystyle S_{k,t}=\sum_{i \in k} s_{i,t}$, for inflation, labour market, economic activity, financial conditions, monetary policy, and macro language. This tests whether predictive power comes from particular parts of the Fed's communication rather than from tone in general.

\textbf{4d — Topic sentiment factors.} Applies PCA to the six topic-specific sentiment series and uses the resulting sentiment factors as regressors. This can prevent collinearity between topic sentiment indicators.

\textbf{4e — Macro-sentiment interactions.} Calculates interaction terms between total sentiment and macro-financial variables, such as $S_t \times unemployment_t$. This tests whether the same tone has different predictive content depending on the economic environment in which it is used.

\textbf{4f — Matched topic interactions.} Calculates topic-specific interaction terms that pair each sentiment channel with its corresponding macro variable, such as inflation sentiment $S_{\pi,t}$ multiplied by inflation deviation $\pi_t-\pi^*$. This tests whether communication is most informative when sentiment about a topic is evaluated in the matching macro context.

\textbf{4g — Novelty.} Calculates the change in aggregate sentiment since the previous meeting, $\bar{s}_t-\bar{s}_{t-1}$. This tests whether revisions in communication matter more than the absolute level of tone.



\textbf{4h — EWMA-smoothed sentiment.} Replaces the raw meeting sentiment input with an exponentially weighted moving average over the current and previous two meetings (window = 3 meetings). This tests whether short-run smoothing reduces noise in the sentiment signal, or instead dilutes the information contained in the most recent communication.

\textbf{4i — Joint PCA (non-nested).} Runs PCA on the combined macro and sentiment dataset rather than adding sentiment after macro factors are formed. Sentiment therefore changes the factor space itself, making the specification non-nested and motivating evaluation with the Diebold-Mariano test.

%  4.3  Main OOS results
% ===========================================================

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:oos_main} reports the best-performing model for each method and architecture. Two statistical tests are used depending on model structure: the Diebold-Mariano (DM) test for joint-PCA models, which are non-nested with the baseline, and the Clark-West (CW) test for nested FAVAR models, where the baseline is a restricted version of the full model. Using DM for nested models would introduce a finite-sample downward bias in the test statistic; using CW for non-nested models would over-reject the null. The two tests are therefore not directly comparable in terms of significance levels.

LLM joint PCA (\texttt{all\_sent}) achieves the lowest overall RMSE (0.243, $+14.1\%$, DM $p = 0.008$) and retains significance through OOS70. LLM nested FAVAR (\texttt{topics}) achieves $+9.9\%$ under CW ($p = 0.001$) and retains significance through OOS80. These two results, using different architectures and different test statistics appropriate to their structure, point to the same conclusion: LLM sentiment carries statistically reliable predictive information about future rate changes beyond the macro baseline. No other method achieves significance under its correct test in the joint-PCA architecture. Gardner \texttt{nest\_matched} passes CW (***) but requires a specific nested architecture with theoretically motivated interaction terms; its joint-PCA variants all show negative DM statistics. Sharpe is at best marginal (*) in both architectures. Even then, its improvement in RMSE is marginal. Similarly, the best Sharpe model (4b2) provides a marginal improvement at low significance.

The interaction and novelty variants (steps 4e--4g) uniformly underperform the simpler specifications for all three methods, consistent with overfitting at $n_{\text{OOS}} = 76$. The one exception is \texttt{Gardner\_nest\_matched}, which benefits from the matched-interaction structure but fails to generalise to joint PCA. Furthermore, the smoothed regressions also underperform showing that adding past sentiment scores "contaminates" the signal and lowers predictability.

%  4.4  Robustness: regime analysis
% ============================================================

\subsection{Robustness Tests}

A natural concern is that the LLM result is driven by a specific economic episode. The OOS period runs from 2016 to 2025 (76 predictions); 20 of these fall in the 2016--2018 period before any named regime begins and are always retained in the leave-one-out samples. The five named economic episodes cover 2019--2025: Pre-COVID (Jan 2019--Feb 2020, $n = 9$), COVID shock (Mar--Jun 2020, $n = 4$), zero lower bound (Jul 2020--Feb 2022, $n = 13$), hiking cycle (Mar 2022--Jul 2023, $n = 12$), and Peak+Cuts (Aug 2023 onward, $n = 18$).

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:regimes} reports leave-one-regime-out DM tests. Excluding any single regime leaves the result significant at ** or ***, with improvements ranging from 11.3\% to 15.5\%. In all cases, the improvement is significant to 5\% proving that the results are valid across different economic regimes.

The Diebold-Mariano test relies on an asymptotic normal approximation that may be
unreliable with only 76 out-of-sample observations. To verify that the significance
result does not depend on this assumption, a moving block bootstrap is applied. The
procedure resamples the series of meeting-by-meeting loss differentials in overlapping   blocks of consecutive observations, preserving the autocorrelation structure of
forecast errors, and repeats this 5,000 times to build an empirical null distribution
of the test statistic. The bootstrapped $p$-value falls below 0.01 across all block
lengths tested ($L \in {3, 4, 5, 8, 9}$), and the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold correction
— which adjusts the DM statistic downward to account for small samples — yields $p =
0.004$ (***). The LLM model's forecasting advantage over the macro baseline is
therefore statistically significant under both parametric and non-parametric
inference.

Furthermore, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:oos_subwindow} shows how LLM performance evolves across other OOS tests. In the main specification, the first forecast is made after the initial 50\% training window and the model is re-estimated before each subsequent meeting. The OOS60, OOS70, OOS80, and OOS90 columns do not retrain after X\%, making it a pure OOS test. The model retains significance at 1\% up to OOS70. The loss of significance in the narrowest windows should therefore be interpreted cautiously: OOS80 and especially OOS90 contain far fewer forecast errors, and the Diebold--Mariano test is heavily penalised by this smaller effective sample even when the RMSE improvement remains economically meaningful. The absolute RMSE is still signficantly below the baseline (-20\% or more) which indicates that the edge has not dissapeared.

\subsection{Communication Regimes and Model Diagnostics}

  Rather than treating FOMC communication as a uniform stream of text, the LLM
  pipeline classifies each sentence along two axes: its linguistic character
  (descriptive versus interpretive) and the nature of any forward commitment
  (conditional versus unconditional). In addition, the LLM flags each meeting
  the presence of elevated uncertainty or asymmetric risk language. This allows the
  forecasting gains documented above to be traced back to specific features of
  central-bank communication.

  The two classification axes form a $2\times 2$ matrix of sentence types, each of
  which can be used in isolation to construct the sentiment input to the best model
  (LLM 4i). Appendix Table~\ref{tab:sent_matrix} reports the out-of-sample RMSE improvement
  relative to the macro baseline for each cell, alongside the full-document reference.

  \paragraph{Descriptive versus interpretive sentences.} As discussed previously,
  descriptive sentences record current economic conditions as facts while
  interpretive sentences convey the committee's assessment, tone, or forward view.
  Using only interpretive sentences to construct the sentiment indicator yields an
  $11.0\%$ RMSE improvement over the macro baseline (DM-$t = 1.23$, $p = 0.110$),
  compared with $14.1\%$ for the full document ($p = 0.008$). The interpretive
  component therefore carries the bulk of the forecasting signal. Descriptive
  sentences entered without interpretive context are actively harmful: the
  descriptive-plus-conditional cell produces a $14.1\%$ degradation in RMSE
  (DM-$t = -3.57$), indicating that stripped-of-tone factual language introduces
  noise rather than signal.

  \paragraph{Delphic versus Odyssean guidance.} Following
  \citet{campbelletal2012}, monetary policy sentences are classified as Delphic
  (conditional on incoming data) or Odyssean (unconditional commitments). This can
  be explored by restricting to sentences labelled as expressing
  \textit{unconditional commitment} (Odyssean) or \textit{none} (Delphic).
  Interpretive sentences with Delphic character preserve an $8.8\%$ improvement
  (DM-$t = 0.96$, $p = 0.168$), while Odyssean-only language reverses the gain to
  $-9.0\%$. This supports the idea that odyssean communication is quickly priced in by the market, leaving no predictive signal for future policy stance. The forecasting signal therefore lives in
  conditional, outlook-based language. It must be noted that only 0.4\% of sentences were labelled as odyssean + interpretive, which may put the statistical validity of these results into question.

  \paragraph{Risk and uncertainty regimes.} Each OOS meeting is classified by
  whether the meeting-window documents exhibit elevated uncertainty (high
  within-document sentiment width) or asymmetric risk language (upward or downward sentiment skew). As explained in Section~\ref{sec:Architecture}, uncertainty is an important measure as it may add predictive value through two core mechanisms. Firstly, the FOMC has an assymetric reaction function, meaning for an equal uncertainty about two opposite scenarios, they will act such to reduce the likelihood of the scenario which they are most concerned about; that is, acting hawkishly to prevent high inflation. Secondly, in periods of high uncertainty, the FOMC may be inclined to retain a neutral stance given that it may risk acting inappropriately and making the situation worse.

  The sentence-level filtering approach works for tense and commitment because nearly
  every sentence receives one of those labels: restricting to interpretive or Delphic
  sentences still leaves a substantial body of text from which to compute sentiment at
  each meeting. Uncertainty and risk language is different: only a small fraction of
  sentences are flagged as expressing elevated uncertainty or asymmetric risk, so
  sentence-level filtering would leave most meetings with too few observations to
  produce a reliable sentiment score.

  The mask procedure therefore operates at the meeting level rather than the sentence
  level. For each meeting, the pipeline counts the total number of sentences flagged for
   elevated uncertainty width, upside skew, and downside skew across all documents in
  the window. These counts are continuous and well-spread across meetings, so each
  meeting receives a binary classification based on a median split: meetings where the
  flag count exceeds the full-sample median (computed across all 151 FOMC meetings) are
  labelled high-uncertainty or high-risk, and the remainder are labelled low. 

  Once sentences have been classified as reflecting elevated uncertainty or skewed risk, the corresponding distribution of policy actions can be examined. Relative to meetings with no uncertainty signal, meetings marked by elevated uncertainty exhibit a higher share of holds (25.5\% compared to 17.2\%), providing evidence consistent with the second mechanism discussed earlier. Meetings characterized by upside risk show an even sharper shift toward tightening, with hikes accounting for 53.2\% of outcomes compared to 31\% among meetings without upside risk. For downside risk, however, the distribution of policy actions changes only marginally.
  
  
  The
  sentiment model itself is then evaluated separately on
  each group of OOS meetings, asking whether its forecasting advantage over the macro
  baseline is larger when the Fed's communication is characterized by more frequent
  uncertainty or risk language. During the 47 high-uncertainty meetings the sentiment model
  improves on the baseline by $17.4\%$, against $11.9\%$ in the remaining 29
  calmer periods. Upward-skew meetings show a $16.6\%$ gain; downward-skew meetings
  show the largest improvement at $20.3\%$. Across all three masks the pattern is
  the same: the model is most useful precisely when macroeconomic signals are most
  ambiguous and the committee is most actively communicating about the distribution
  of risks rather than a central-case forecast.

\section{Discussion \& Conclusion}
This paper asks whether sentiment extracted from FOMC communication improves forecasts of the next-period policy stance once standard macro-financial information is taken into account. The answer is cautiously affirmative. The strongest LLM-based specification delivers a substantial reduction in out-of-sample RMSE relative to the macro-only benchmark, and this improvement remains visible across the robustness checks considered in the analysis. By contrast, the dictionary-based approaches provide only marginal gains, which are often statistically insignificant. This contrast suggests that the predictive content of central bank communication is not well captured by simple word counts alone. Instead, it appears to depend on contextual features of language that distinguish, for example, a mechanical description of current conditions from an interpretation of risks, uncertainty, or the reaction function.

Several more specific findings follow from the empirical exercise. First, the results show how easily communication-based forecasting models can overfit. Specifications with many interactions generally perform worse out of sample, even when they appear intuitively appealing. This implies that the information in FOMC language is useful but relatively sparse, and that flexible model designs can quickly transform signal into noise. Second, smoothing sentiment indicators does not improve performance. In this setting, smoothing appears to contaminate the timing of the signal by blending information across meetings, which is costly when the objective is to forecast the next policy decision using only information available at the forecast date. Third, minutes appear especially informative in some specifications, although this result should be interpreted with care: it may reflect the genuine richness of the minutes, but it may also partly reflect the LLM's greater ability to extract structured sentiment from longer and more explanatory documents. In practice, the best results still come from using the full communication set rather than relying on a single document type.

The label-based results provide further insight into why the LLM approach is useful. The most informative sentences are not simply those that mention policy directly, but those that are Delphic and interpretive: sentences in which policymakers explain how they view the state of the economy, the balance of risks, or the conditions under which policy may change. This supports the interpretation that FOMC communication has predictive value because it reveals aspects of the Committee's assessment and reaction function rather than because it mechanically announces future decisions. The regime results point in the same direction. Forecast improvements are strongest in high-risk and high-uncertainty environments, precisely when macroeconomic indicators are harder to interpret and communication may provide additional guidance about how policymakers weigh competing risks.

These conclusions are nevertheless subject to important limitations. The first concerns the quality and scale of the training data used to construct the LLM labels. Although the annotation process was designed to be as consistent as possible, labelling central bank communication is inherently difficult: many sentences are ambiguous, context-dependent, or simultaneously contain hawkish and dovish elements. This challenge limited the size of the hand-labelled dataset to slightly below 1,000 sentences, which is smaller than would be ideal for such a nuanced classification task. As a result, the labelling scheme also had to be less ambitious than originally intended. For instance, the sentiment label was initially designed to contain five categories, which would have allowed a more granular measurement of tail risk and intensity, but the available training data did not support that level of complexity reliably.

A second limitation is the scarcity of regression observations in some of the conditional exercises. The full sample contains a limited number of FOMC meetings, and this constraint becomes more severe when the data are split into regimes such as high uncertainty, skewed risk, or Odyssean communication. The resulting estimates are therefore informative but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence about each regime in isolation. Rather, they should be read as suggestive patterns that are consistent with the broader finding of the paper: communication is most valuable when it helps interpret uncertainty, risks, and the likely policy reaction to evolving macroeconomic conditions.

\section{Shadow Rate: Theoretical Background}
  \label{app:shadow-rate}

  When the nominal policy rate approaches its effective lower bound (ELB),
  conventional interest rate measures lose their ability to capture the full stance
  of monetary policy. The \textit{shadow rate} is a latent variable that extends the
  policyeratedconcept(below+the ELB,ymeasuringftheahypothetical rate that would
  prevail in the absence of the non-negativity constraint.

  \paragraph{Black's option interpretation.}
  \citet{black1995interest} observed that a nominal bond is analogous to a call
  option: the holder can always choose to hold physical currency yielding zero, so
  the observed short rate $r_t$ satisfies
  \begin{equation}
      r_t = \max\!\bigl(s_t,\; r^{\min}\bigr),
      \label{eq:shadow}
  \end{equation}
  where $s_t$ is the unobserved shadow rate and $r^{\min}$ is the effective lower
  bound. The shadow rate is unconstrained and can take any real value; $r_t$ is its
  censored, observable counterpart.

  \paragraph{Term-structure identification.}
  Because $s_t$ is latent, it must be recovered from the yield curve. Under an
  affine shadow-rate term-structure model (SRTSM), long-maturity yields embed
  market expectations of future short rates, which depend on $s_t$ even when $r_t$
  is pinned at the ELB. \citet{wuxia2016} fit a three-factor Gaussian SRTSM
  to U.S.\ Treasury yields. The shadow rate is parameterised as
  \begin{equation}
      s_t = \mathbf{a}' X_t + b,
      \label{eq:srtsm}
  \end{equation}
  where $X_t$ is a vector of latent pricing factors and $\mathbf{a}, b$ are
  estimated by maximum likelihood, so that equation~\eqref{eq:shadow} holds at
  every point in the sample. The implied $s_t$ is then extracted by inverting the
  fitted model.

  \paragraph{Relevance for policy measurement.}
  During the 2009--2015 and 2020--2022 ZLB episodes the effective federal funds
  rate was compressed to 0--25 basis points, masking substantial variation in the
  underlying policy stance driven by forward guidance and asset purchases. The
  Wu--Xia shadow rate declines to approximately $-3\%$ in 2014 and $-2\%$ in 2021,
  capturing this accommodation in a single continuous series. Using $\Delta s_t$
  rather than $\Delta r_t^{\text{eff}}$ as the dependent variable in this thesis
  ensures that the model is evaluated on a measure of policy stance that is
  informative throughout the sample, rather than a censored outcome during ZLB
  periods.

\section{Baseline Model Comparison}
\label{app:baseline-comparison}

As a robustness check, the baseline selection was repeated using simpler forecasting rules. The naive benchmark predicts no change in the policy stance, while the market benchmark uses the difference between the implied federal funds rate and the current shadow rate as the expected policy change. For the main shadow-rate-change dependent variable, these alternatives underperform the FAVAR baseline. When the dependent variable is instead defined as the change in the effective federal funds rate, the market benchmark obtains a lower RMSE than the naive and FAVAR baselines, but the improvement is not statistically significant. This supports using the FAVAR as the main benchmark while retaining the shadow-rate target as the preferred measure of policy stance.

  Before adopting the macro-only FAVAR as the benchmark against which
  sentiment-augmented models are evaluated, three no-sentiment forecasting
  strategies were considered as candidates for the baseline role.

  \paragraph{Candidate baselines.}

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Naive-zero.} Always forecast $\Delta r_{t+1} = 0$.
          This exploits the empirical regularity that the Federal Open Market
          Committee (FOMC) holds rates unchanged at the majority of meetings,
          making zero the modal outcome over the sample.

    \item \textbf{Market (Kuttner spread).} Use the spread
          $\widehat{\Delta r}_{t+1} = \text{implied FFR}_t - \text{FFR}_t$
          directly as the forecast, where the implied rate is derived from
          federal funds futures following \citet{kuttner2001}.
          This is the pure market expectation of the next-meeting rate
          change and requires no estimated parameters.

    \item \textbf{Macro FAVAR.} An expanding-window OLS regression of
          $\Delta r_{t+1}$ on the first principal components of seven macro
          series (inflation deviation from target, unemployment gap, implied
          FFR, effective rate, GDP growth, VIX, and CFNAI), retaining enough
          components to explain 90\% of variance. The expanding window
          initialises at 50\% of the sample and retrains at each step,
          producing one-step-ahead pseudo-out-of-sample forecasts.
  \end{enumerate}

  Macro FAVAR  & 0.2920 & $-6.1\%$  & 0.3211 & 0.3210 & 0.3729 & 0.2250 \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{table}

  The market spread is the most accurate of the three (RMSE 0.2606),
  followed by naive-zero (0.2753), with the macro FAVAR performing worst
  (0.2920). Diebold--Mariano tests \citep{dieboldmariano1995} find no
  statistically significant difference between any pair of models at
  conventional levels (all $|t| < 1.645$, $p > 0.10$); the ranking is
  therefore not distinguishable from noise.

  \paragraph{Sensitivity to the choice of dependent variable.}

  The ranking above reverses when the dependent variable is switched from
  the raw FFR to the Wu--Xia shadow rate \citep{wuxia2016}, which this thesis
  uses as the primary measure of the monetary policy stance. Under that DV
  the macro FAVAR achieves RMSE 0.2826, beating both the market spread and
  naive-zero, while the market spread becomes the worst performer. The
  inversion is economically intuitive: the Kuttner futures spread captures
  discrete, round-number FOMC decisions and is therefore informative about
  raw FFR changes but less so about the shadow rate, which moves
  continuously even at the zero lower bound. The macro FAVAR, by contrast,
  incorporates real-activity and financial-condition variables that track
  the ZLB regime and picks up shadow-rate dynamics that the futures market
  cannot price in the conventional sense.

  Given that the shadow rate is the conceptually appropriate policy
  indicator for a sample spanning two ZLB episodes (2008--2015 and
  2020--2022), the macro FAVAR is retained as the baseline for all
  sentiment-augmented comparisons in the main analysis.
